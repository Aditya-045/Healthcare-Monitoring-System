# Liver Disease — Reference Notes

## Overview
The liver performs hundreds of functions, including filtering toxins,
producing proteins needed for blood clotting, and processing bilirubin, a
byproduct of red blood cell breakdown. Liver disease markers are usually
grouped into "liver function tests," which look at how well the liver is
clearing waste products (like bilirubin) and whether liver cells are
being damaged (reflected in enzyme levels).

## Total Bilirubin and Direct Bilirubin
Bilirubin is produced when old red blood cells break down and is normally
processed by the liver and excreted. "Total bilirubin" combines direct
(conjugated, already processed by the liver) and indirect bilirubin.
Elevated total bilirubin, especially with a high direct fraction, often
points to the liver struggling to process and excrete it — visible
clinically as jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes).

## Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)
Alkaline phosphatase is an enzyme found in the liver and bile ducts, among
other tissues. Elevated levels can suggest bile flow is obstructed or that
the bile ducts are inflamed, though it can also rise for reasons unrelated
to the liver, so it is generally interpreted alongside the other markers
here rather than alone.

## Alamine Aminotransferase (ALT / SGPT)
ALT is an enzyme concentrated mostly in liver cells. When liver cells are
damaged or inflamed, ALT leaks into the bloodstream, making it one of the
most specific and commonly used indicators of liver cell injury.

## Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST / SGOT)
AST is similar to ALT but is also found in muscle and heart tissue, so it
is somewhat less liver-specific on its own. It is usually interpreted
alongside ALT; the ratio between the two can offer additional diagnostic
clues (for example, certain patterns are more typical of alcohol-related
liver injury than others).

## Total Proteins and Albumin
The liver manufactures most of the proteins circulating in blood, including
albumin. Total protein and albumin levels reflect the liver's synthetic
(production) function rather than damage directly — low levels can suggest
the liver's ability to produce proteins has been impaired, which tends to
happen with more advanced or chronic liver disease rather than acute
injury.

## Albumin and Globulin Ratio (A/G Ratio)
Globulins are a separate group of blood proteins, some produced by the
liver and some by the immune system. The albumin-to-globulin ratio can
shift in chronic liver disease as albumin production falls and/or globulin
production rises (for example, due to associated inflammation), making an
abnormally low ratio a useful supporting signal.

## Age and Gender Considerations
Liver disease risk and presentation can differ somewhat between men and
women, and risk generally increases with age due to cumulative exposure to
risk factors (alcohol use, viral hepatitis, metabolic conditions, certain
medications). These demographic fields provide useful context for
interpreting the lab values above, though they are not diagnostic on their
own.

## General Note on Interpretation
As with any lab panel, no single liver marker should be interpreted in
isolation. Clinicians typically look at the overall pattern — which values
are elevated or low, and by how much — together with the patient's
symptoms and history. Any abnormal result here should be discussed with a
healthcare professional rather than self-interpreted as a diagnosis.
