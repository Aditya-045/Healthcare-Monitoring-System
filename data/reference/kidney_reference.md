# Chronic Kidney Disease — Reference Notes

## Overview
Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a long-term condition in which the kidneys
gradually lose their ability to filter waste and excess fluid from the
blood. It is often diagnosed and tracked using a combination of blood
pressure readings, urine tests, and blood tests rather than any single
number. Early stages can have no noticeable symptoms, which is why routine
lab panels are important for people with risk factors such as diabetes or
hypertension.

## Blood Pressure (bp)
Blood pressure measures the force of blood against artery walls. Sustained
high blood pressure (hypertension) is both a cause and a consequence of
kidney disease, since damaged kidneys are less able to regulate fluid and
sodium balance, which in turn raises blood pressure further. A reading
consistently above roughly 130/80 mm/Hg is generally considered elevated.

## Blood Urea (bu)
Urea is a waste product formed when the body breaks down protein. Healthy
kidneys filter urea out of the blood efficiently. When kidney function
declines, urea builds up in the bloodstream, so an elevated blood urea
level is a common early signal of reduced kidney filtering capacity.

## Serum Creatinine (sc)
Creatinine is a waste product from normal muscle activity. Like urea, it is
normally cleared by the kidneys at a fairly constant rate, which makes it
one of the most widely used markers of kidney function. Rising creatinine
levels typically indicate that the kidneys are filtering blood less
effectively than before.

## Sodium (sod) and Potassium (pot)
The kidneys play a central role in keeping sodium and potassium within a
narrow healthy range, since both are essential for nerve and muscle
function, including the heart. In advanced kidney disease, potassium in
particular can rise to dangerous levels because the kidneys can no longer
excrete it efficiently, and sodium balance can become erratic, contributing
to fluid retention and swelling.

## Hemoglobin (hemo), Packed Cell Volume (pcv), Red Blood Cell Count (rc)
These three values all relate to the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.
Kidneys produce a hormone that stimulates red blood cell production; when
kidney function drops, this hormone decreases, often leading to anemia —
seen as lower hemoglobin, lower packed cell volume, and a lower red blood
cell count. Anemia is a very common accompaniment of moderate-to-advanced
CKD.

## White Blood Cell Count (wc)
White blood cells are part of the immune system. An elevated count can
suggest infection or inflammation, which is worth noting since infections
can both stem from and further stress compromised kidney function.

## Urine Specific Gravity (sg)
This measures how concentrated urine is compared to water. Kidneys that
are functioning well can concentrate or dilute urine as needed; a
persistently low specific gravity can indicate the kidneys are losing this
regulatory ability.

## Albumin in Urine (al) and Sugar in Urine (su)
Healthy kidneys keep protein (albumin) largely out of urine, so its
presence is a hallmark sign of kidney damage — the higher the level, the
more significant the damage tends to be. Sugar (glucose) in urine is
unusual outside of diabetes and can indicate that blood sugar levels are
high enough to spill over into urine, a common contributor to kidney
damage over time.

## Hypertension (htn), Diabetes Mellitus (dm), Coronary Artery Disease (cad)
These are the three most significant risk factors and comorbidities for
CKD. Diabetes and high blood pressure together account for the majority of
CKD cases worldwide, and coronary artery disease shares many of the same
underlying vascular risk factors.

## Appetite (appet), Pedal Edema (pe), Anemia (ane)
Poor appetite, swelling in the legs/feet (pedal edema), and anemia are
common clinical signs that tend to appear as kidney function declines
further, reflecting the buildup of waste products and fluid, and reduced
red blood cell production, respectively.

## General Note on Interpretation
No single marker above should be read in isolation. CKD is typically
staged using a combination of these values together with the estimated
glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculated from age, sex, and creatinine.
Any single abnormal value warrants a conversation with a healthcare
professional rather than a self-diagnosis.
